A Note from Kent and Pamela

Have you ever noticed how easy it is to become an expert on everyone else's life?

We can often see exactly what our spouse, our children, our friends, or the person driving in front of us ought to be doing differently.

But when it comes to understanding ourselves, things get a little more complicated.

We know our names, our birthdays, our favorite foods, and probably a few things we'd rather forget. We know what makes us laugh, what irritates us, and which family gatherings require a little extra patience.

But how well do we really know ourselves?

Why do certain situations bring out the best in us while others bring out reactions we don't particularly like?

Why do we sometimes repeat the same mistakes, even when we know better?

And why is it so much easier to recognize someone else's peculiar habits than our own?

These are some of the questions that make being human so fascinating.

We wrote this little book because we believe that life is an adventure worth exploring, and one of the most interesting discoveries we can make is learning more about ourselves.

You won't find a lot of complicated theories here. We're not going to ask you to memorize psychological terms or spend hours examining every decision you've ever made.

Instead, we're inviting you to slow down, smile occasionally, and become a little more curious about the person you've been living with your entire life.

You.

And here's something we'd like you to remember as you read:

You don't have to take yourself so seriously to take your life seriously.

In fact, learning to laugh at ourselves may be one of the healthiest places to begin.

So make yourself comfortable.

Let's see what we can discover together.

Kent and Pamela

Chapter One — Welcome to the Mystery Called You

You're more interesting than you think.

Let's begin with a rather unusual thought.

You are the only person who has been present for every single moment of your life.

Think about that.

Every birthday. Every disappointment. Every accomplishment. Every embarrassing moment. Every decision—good, bad, and questionable.

You were there for all of it.

And yet, after all those years of living with yourself, there may still be some things about you that you don't understand.

Isn't that interesting?

The Person in the Mirror

Most of us have a fairly comfortable explanation of who we are.

We might say:

“I'm just a quiet person.”

“I've always been independent.”

“I'm not very good at expressing my feelings.”

“I've never been very patient.”

Or perhaps the all-purpose explanation:

“That's just the way I am.”

Now, sometimes those statements describe something real about us.

But sometimes they're simply stories we've been telling ourselves for so long that we've stopped wondering whether they're entirely true.

What if there's more to the story?

What if the quiet person has something important to say?

What if the independent person secretly wishes someone would offer a helping hand?

What if the impatient person is actually afraid of losing control?

And what if the person who says, “That's just the way I am,” hasn't yet discovered all the ways they could be?

We aren't suggesting that every personality trait needs to change.

Far from it.

We're simply suggesting that curiosity can open doors that certainty sometimes keeps closed.

We Are Delightfully Complicated

Human beings are wonderfully inconsistent.

We want adventure, but we also want security.

We want to be understood, but we don't always explain ourselves very well.

We want people to be honest with us, provided they choose their words very carefully.

We want to grow, but we'd prefer that growth not involve too much discomfort.

And we want to be patient, preferably immediately.

If you recognize yourself in any of those statements, welcome to the human race.

You're in excellent company.

One of the most freeing discoveries we can make is that being human involves contradictions.

We can be loving and occasionally selfish.

Generous and sometimes fearful.

Confident in one situation and uncertain in another.

None of that makes us hopelessly flawed.

It makes us interesting.

And it gives us something worth exploring.

Curiosity Instead of Criticism

Imagine two different ways of responding to something you don't like about yourself.

The first sounds like this:

“Why do I always do that? What's wrong with me?”

The second sounds like this:

“Well, that was interesting. I wonder why I reacted that way?”

Both responses recognize that something happened.

But they lead us in very different directions.

The first tends to produce defensiveness, embarrassment, or discouragement.

The second creates an opening.

An opportunity to learn.

And perhaps even an opportunity to change.

This is one of the central ideas we'd like you to carry throughout this book:

You can become curious about yourself without becoming critical of yourself.

You can notice a habit without condemning yourself for having it.

You can acknowledge a mistake without allowing it to define you.

And you can discover something surprising about yourself without immediately deciding you need a complete personality renovation.

Sometimes the most helpful response is simply:

“Huh. I never noticed that before.”

That's a wonderful beginning.

The Adventure of Paying Attention

Most of us spend our days moving from one responsibility to the next.

We work, drive, shop, cook, answer messages, take care of people, and try to remember where we put our glasses.

Meanwhile, an entire world of thoughts, feelings, assumptions, and reactions is unfolding inside us.

And much of the time, we hardly notice.

What would happen if we began paying a little more attention?

Not obsessively.

Not critically.

Just curiously.

We might begin to notice what energizes us.

What makes us uncomfortable.

Which people help us feel most like ourselves.

What situations bring out our generosity, creativity, or courage.

And which situations cause us to retreat, become defensive, or say something we later wish we'd phrased differently.

Each discovery gives us another small piece of the puzzle.

And the remarkable thing about a puzzle is that we don't need to see the entire picture to enjoy finding the next piece.

You Don't Need to Have It All Figured Out

There's a certain freedom in admitting that we don't completely understand ourselves.

It means we're still learning.

Still growing.

Still capable of being surprised.

And perhaps that's one of the nicest things about being alive.

No matter how many birthdays we've celebrated, there may still be wonderful things waiting to be discovered.

A new interest.

A different perspective.

A healthier way of responding.

A deeper connection with someone we love.

Or simply the ability to laugh at something that once made us terribly upset.

So here's our invitation as we begin this journey together:

Don't worry about becoming a different person.

Start by becoming a little more curious about the person you already are.

You might be pleasantly surprised by what you discover.

Something to Wonder About

Find a quiet moment, perhaps with a cup of coffee, and consider these questions:

1. What's something about yourself that still surprises you? Maybe it's a habit, a reaction, or an unexpected interest.

2. What's one thing you've always said about yourself that might deserve a second look? Perhaps an old label doesn't tell the whole story.

3. When was the last time you laughed at yourself? What happened, and did laughing make the experience a little easier?

There's no test at the end of this chapter. No grades, no right answers, and certainly no need to share anything you're not comfortable sharing.

Just notice what comes to mind.

A little curiosity can be the beginning of a wonderful discovery.

Chapter Two — Funny How We Do That

The peculiar habits and contradictions we all share.

Have you ever noticed that people can be perfectly reasonable about almost everything—until they aren't? We can manage complicated responsibilities and offer remarkably sensible advice. Then we spend twenty minutes looking for our glasses while they're sitting on top of our heads.

It's a curious business, being human. And sometimes, it's downright funny.

The Things We Do

You decide to eat healthier. Then someone brings homemade cookies to the office. Well, it would be rude not to have one.

You promise yourself you'll be more patient. Then the person ahead of you has seventeen items in the express lane. Apparently, patience has limits.

You decide to listen more carefully to your spouse. But halfway through the conversation, you've already prepared a brilliant response to something they haven't finished saying.

Most of us have good intentions. But somewhere between our intentions and our actions, something interesting happens. We become ourselves—the real version, who occasionally makes a mountain out of something that wasn't even a respectable hill.

We All Have Our Little Stories

When we're late, there was unexpected traffic. When someone else is late, they need to manage their time better. When we interrupt, we're enthusiastic. When someone interrupts us, they're rude.

We can be remarkably generous in explaining our own behavior and remarkably efficient at judging someone else's. Perhaps the next time someone irritates us, we might ask: What might be going on in their life that I don't know about?

The Trouble With Being Right

Have you ever found yourself arguing about which road to take, how to load the dishwasher, or whether something happened Tuesday or Wednesday? At some point, the original issue becomes irrelevant. Now we're defending our credibility.

And sometimes, if we're fortunate, we realize how ridiculous the whole thing has become. We laugh. The tension breaks. And the dishwasher gets loaded, one way or another.

The ability to laugh at ourselves gives us room to be wrong without feeling diminished.

Catch Yourself Being Human

When we can laugh at ourselves, we don't have to spend so much energy protecting an image of perfection. We can relax, listen better, admit a mistake, and extend a little more grace.

The next time you catch yourself doing something inconsistent, don't rush to judge it. Just notice. Maybe you insist you're flexible, provided everyone agrees to your plan.

Instead of saying, 'What's wrong with me?' try, 'Well, isn't that interesting?' The goal isn't to become a person without quirks. That would be terribly boring.

Something to Wonder About

1. What's one of your funny little habits?

2. When have you caught yourself doing the very thing that annoys you in someone else?

3. What's something you used to be certain about that you now see differently?

Chapter Three — The Stories We Tell Ourselves

How our experiences shape what we believe.

Have you ever told a family story so many times that everyone knows exactly how it goes—even the people who weren't there? We don't just tell stories about what happened. We tell stories about who we are.

Sometimes those stories become so familiar that we forget they're stories at all.

Once Upon a Time, There Was You

Perhaps you've always thought of yourself as shy, independent, or the responsible one. Where did those ideas come from? A teacher's comment? A parent's description? One difficult experience?

A few encouraging words can give someone confidence for a lifetime. But a label can also become a limitation. Someone called 'the strong one' may believe they aren't allowed to need help.

The Invisible Rulebook

Every family has unwritten rules. Some say to speak your mind; others say not to make a fuss. Some show affection with hugs; others make sure your tires are properly inflated before a long drive.

We carry those rules into adulthood, often without noticing. Then we meet someone whose family had a completely different rulebook. No wonder relationships can be complicated.

We can appreciate what was helpful, reconsider what wasn't, and decide what we'd like to carry forward.

A Chapter Isn't the Whole Story

Imagine someone who had an embarrassing experience speaking in school and has said ever since, 'I'm terrible at public speaking.' One moment has become a lifelong conclusion.

What if the story changed to: 'I had an embarrassing experience, and I've been nervous about it ever since'? The first sounds permanent. The second leaves room for possibility.

A chapter of your life doesn't have to become the title of your entire story.

You're Still Writing

We can't rewrite everything that happened to us. But we can reconsider what those experiences mean, recognize strengths we hadn't noticed, and choose how to approach the next chapter.

You are more than the labels you've been given and the stories you've told yourself. There's still more to discover.

Something to Wonder About

1. What's something you've always believed about yourself? When did that belief begin?

2. What was one unwritten rule in your family growing up?

3. Is there an old description of yourself that deserves a second look?

Chapter Four — Who Pushed My Buttons?

Discovering what our reactions reveal.

Have you ever been having a perfectly good day when someone says four innocent-sounding words: 'We need to talk'? Suddenly your mind is racing. What did I do? What's wrong?

And sometimes the person simply wanted to know what you'd like for dinner. Isn't it amazing how quickly we can go from peaceful to panicked without leaving the room?

Everybody Has Buttons

A spouse, a child, a coworker—or the driver going ten miles below the speed limit in the passing lane—can push one of ours. Something that bothers us terribly may not bother someone else at all.

Why? Because our reactions aren't just about what's happening around us. Sometimes they're about what's happening inside us.

Same Words, Different Meanings

Someone says, 'You seem awfully quiet today.' One person thinks, 'How thoughtful.' Another thinks, 'What's wrong with being quiet?' Same words. Different experiences.

Our expectations, our experiences, and the stories we tell ourselves shape what we hear. And sometimes we're simply tired or hungry. Not every reaction requires deep analysis.

The Pause

We can't control every comment or disappointment. But we can learn to notice what happens inside us before we respond.

Why did that bother me? Am I reacting to what happened—or what I think it means? What if I waited a moment?

We won't always remember to pause. Even noticing afterward can teach us something.

A Little Grace

Sometimes our reactions point to a desire for respect, belonging, or security. Those needs aren't wrong. The question is what we do when they feel threatened.

And the people around us have buttons too. A suggestion we intended as helpful may sound critical. Curiosity can accomplish more than a convincing argument.

The goal isn't to become someone who never gets upset. It's to understand a little more about why.

Something to Wonder About

1. What tends to push your buttons?

2. What might be underneath a strong reaction—respect, appreciation, belonging, or understanding?

3. When have you misunderstood someone's intentions?

Chapter Five — The People Who Helped Make Us

Understanding the influence of relationships.

Have you ever heard yourself say something and realized you sounded exactly like your mother or father? Maybe it was a reminder to turn off the lights because electricity isn't free.

We spend much of our lives becoming our own people, only to discover that we're carrying little pieces of other people around with us. We don't become who we are all by ourselves.

The People Who Leave Their Mark

Parents, grandparents, teachers, coaches, friends, neighbors—sometimes even someone who was in our life only briefly—can influence us deeply.

A teacher noticed a talent. A friend encouraged us at just the right moment. A stranger offered kindness on a difficult day. They may not remember. But we do.

Lessons We Never Knew We Were Learning

We learn how to handle disappointment, express affection, and deal with disagreements by watching others. Some lessons are wonderful; others deserve a second look.

Perhaps you learned to help neighbors. Perhaps you learned to say 'I'm fine' even when you weren't. We can appreciate what helped us and reconsider what didn't.

Gifts We Overlook

Not every important gift comes wrapped in paper. Some arrive as patience, encouragement, or someone showing up when it would have been easier not to.

And you may have offered that same gift to someone else without realizing it. A small word of encouragement can become a lasting memory.

What We Carry Forward

Not every influence was easy. Some words hurt, and some experiences taught lessons we'd rather not have needed. We don't have to pretend those things were good.

But we can choose what to carry forward and what we'd rather leave behind. We're still influencing one another today, in the ordinary moments that seem too small to remember.

We are shaped by our relationships, and we help shape the lives of others. That's a remarkable opportunity.

Something to Wonder About

1. Who made a positive difference in your life?

2. What habit or belief did you pick up from someone close to you?

3. What quality do you hope people experience when they're around you?

Chapter Six — Permission to Be Imperfect

Learning to laugh at ourselves.

Have you ever met someone who seems to have everything together? Their house is neat, their schedule organized, and they look cheerful before their first cup of coffee.

Meanwhile, the rest of us are searching for our keys and wondering what happened to Tuesday. Welcome to being human. It's wonderful, but it can be a little messy.

The Myth of Having It All Together

Somewhere along the way, many of us decided we should make good decisions, say the right things, manage our emotions, and never have spinach stuck in our teeth during an important conversation.

But we're human. We forget, misunderstand, and occasionally make the same mistake twice. Sometimes three times, just to be certain.

Permission to Be a Beginner

The desire to get everything right can keep us from trying something new. What if we gave ourselves permission to ask questions, be beginners, and learn by making mistakes?

We all had to learn to walk. Most of us fell down along the way. Fortunately, nobody expected a written explanation.

The Gift of a Good Laugh

Humor isn't a substitute for responsibility. Some mistakes call for an apology or a change. But responsibility doesn't require us to spend our lives feeling terrible about every imperfect moment.

We can take responsibility for our mistakes without allowing them to define us.

What Would You Tell a Friend?

If a friend made a mistake, would you call them hopeless? Probably not. You'd offer understanding, encouragement, maybe even a story about your own spectacular blunder.

Why do we sometimes speak to ourselves in ways we'd never speak to someone we love? Personal growth isn't about instant perfection. It's about awareness, another choice, and trying again.

Give yourself permission to be a work in progress. You don't have to be perfect to enjoy the adventure.

Something to Wonder About

1. When are you hardest on yourself?

2. What's something you learned to do after being bad at it at first?

3. What embarrassing moment makes you laugh now?

Chapter Seven — What If There's More?

The adventure of discovering what might still be possible.

Have you ever tried something new and surprised yourself? Perhaps you made an unexpected friend or handled a difficult moment differently than you once would have.

And afterward you thought, 'Well, I didn't know I had that in me.' What if there are still things about yourself waiting to be discovered?

The Person You're Still Becoming

We know our routines, opinions, and favorite restaurants. But familiarity can make us less curious. We begin to assume that the person we are today is the person we'll always be.

What if there's more courage, patience, or creativity in us than we've discovered? Or a little more willingness to let someone else choose the restaurant? That could be a breakthrough.

Small Discoveries

Personal growth isn't always dramatic. Sometimes we listen a little longer, recognize an old habit, apologize sooner, or ask a question instead of making an assumption.

Small discoveries can change the way we experience our lives and the people around us. We don't have to reinvent ourselves. We can simply remain open.

The Adventure Continues

We've laughed at our habits, considered the stories we tell, wondered about our reactions, remembered those who shaped us, and given ourselves permission to be imperfect.

This little book was never meant to give you all the answers. We're still discovering things ourselves. We simply hope you'll approach your life with more curiosity, kindness, and humor.

You are an interesting person. Your experiences and relationships matter. And the possibilities ahead may be greater than you imagine.

Take your life seriously enough to explore it, but not so seriously that you forget to enjoy it.

A Final Thought from Kent and Pamela

That's the spirit of Sojourn. Not becoming someone else, but discovering more of who you are—and what might still be possible.

Thank you for sharing a little of your journey with us. We hope you'll keep discovering.

Kent and Pamela Hafemann
